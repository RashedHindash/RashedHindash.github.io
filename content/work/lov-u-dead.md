*Lov U Dead* is a 13 minute psychological thriller directed by Nitesh Marwaha,
written by Hysum Ismail and produced by Light Craft Films in Dubai. A composed
mother prepares to tell her two young children that their father has died.

I animated the fly.

- **[The film on Vimeo](https://vimeo.com/1164268136/6b75ca31e5)**
- **[IMDb](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt40738882/)**

## The brief, and the clock

I came onto this last minute, with roughly a day to animate a handful of shots
using the fly model and rig that had already been built.

Everything around me was locked. The plates were shot, so the timing, the
camera, the pans and the moves were all fixed before I arrived. That is a very
different job from animating into an empty scene. The performance had to live
inside decisions that had already been made, which narrows the problem
considerably and makes the remaining choices matter more.

Within that, the fly was the instrument for directing attention. In several
shots it is the thing carrying the viewer's eye through the frame, so its path
was not really a question of what a fly would plausibly do. It was a question of
where the audience needed to be looking and when, with the fly as the means of
getting them there.

## The real constraint was time, not difficulty

The shots were not complicated. What made this hard was that a day is a day.

There are a few I would have taken further given the chance. The flying shot
wanted more polish. So did the hands while the fly cleans itself, which is the
kind of small, fussy, character-carrying detail that rewards iteration and I
simply did not have room to iterate on. It reads, but it could have been more
interesting in how it was presented, and I know roughly what I would have done
with another pass.

That is worth writing down rather than leaving out. Most of what gets published
is the version that had enough time. This one is the version that had a day, and
it is useful to be able to say which parts of it I would spend a second day on.

## Pulling back on exaggeration

The other challenge was a habit rather than a deadline.

My instinct with creatures is to push things further than life does. Exaggeration
is usually the right instinct, and here it was not, because the fly had to sit
inside live-action plates alongside a performance that is quiet and serious. Push
it too far and it stops being an insect in a room and becomes a cartoon in a
room, which would have pulled against the entire tone of the film.

The line I had to hold was between two competing requirements. The silhouette
needed to stay clear, since the fly is small and motion blurred in some shots, and
an unclear shape at that scale simply disappears. But the clarity could not come
from the usual route of pushing poses and spacing harder, because that would
have broken the realism the film depends on.

What that leaves is a narrower toolkit: cleaner arcs, more deliberate holds where
the fly settles, and letting the staging and the contrast against the plate do
work that exaggeration would normally do. It is a more restrained way of solving
readability than I would reach for by default, and it was the most useful thing
this job taught me.

Overall it was a good experience and genuinely enjoyable. Short deadlines are
clarifying: with a day, you find out quite quickly which decisions were actually
load-bearing.
