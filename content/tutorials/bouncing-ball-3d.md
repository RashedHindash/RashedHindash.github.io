This is the same exercise as the 2D bounce, deliberately. Repeating a problem
you have already solved is the cheapest way to learn a piece of software,
because when something goes wrong you know it is the tool and not the idea.

## What the video covers

Opening 3ds Max for the first time and learning to move around in it: orbiting,
panning, zooming, and switching views. Then creating an object and a ground
plane, finding the timeline again in its 3D form, and animating the ball
bouncing on the spot with squash and stretch.

## Why it is worth repeating the exercise

**Navigation is a skill, not a preliminary.** Most early frustration in 3D is
not about animation at all. It is about not being able to get the camera where
you want it, which quietly makes every other task harder. Spending real time on
orbit, pan and zoom now saves considerably more later.

**A ground plane gives you something to be wrong against.** In 2D the floor was
implied. Here it is an object, and the moment the ball intersects it or floats
above it the error is obvious. That visibility is useful.

**Three dimensions add an axis you did not have to think about.** A bounce that
looked correct from the front can be drifting in depth. Checking the animation
from more than one view becomes part of the routine from this point onward.

## What to watch for

- **Check from the side and the front.** Motion that reads from one angle can be wrong from another.
- **Contact with the plane.** The ball should touch it, not sink through it or hover.
- **Deformation axis.** Squash flattens toward the surface it hits, which means the direction matters once the ball is in space.
- **Frame rate and timing.** The same number of frames now has to hold up against a real ground plane and a real camera.
