The same job as the previous sheet, solved at the other end of the dial. Where
Tagger simplifies, this one commits to observation.

## What the video covers

The design of a male detective character in a realistic register: the costume
and the reasoning behind each garment, the hero assets and weapons he carries,
and the same presentation layout used for the stylised sheet. Recorded start to
finish, sped up, with no commentary.

## Realism is a constraint, not an absence of style

It is tempting to treat realism as the default that you get by not stylising.
The opposite is true. Stylisation grants permission to invent; realism removes
it and replaces it with research.

Once the figure is proportioned like an actual person, every element has to
survive the audience's own experience of the world. Fabric has to fall the way
fabric falls, leather has to crease where a body creases it, straps have to
carry load along a line where load could actually travel. The audience will not
be able to articulate why something is wrong, but they will know, because they
have spent their whole lives looking at people wearing clothes.

## The costume has to argue for the character's job

The jacket is brown leather with panelled shoulders and a reinforced upper
sleeve. It is worn open over a plain white tee. The trousers are dark, with
knee panels and pouches strapped to the thigh. The shoes are brown leather
dress shoes.

That last item is the one worth stopping on. Everything above the ankle suggests
someone equipped for physical trouble. The shoes suggest someone who is expected
in an office. Together they say plainclothes: a man who works inside an
institution but goes into the field, and who is not issued a uniform. Give him
combat boots and he becomes a soldier. The design is carried by a single
inexpensive decision at the bottom of the frame.

The same logic runs through the smaller notes. The earpiece and its visible wire
place him inside an organisation with communications. The forearm wrap says the
job has recently been physical. The jacket is worn rather than new, because a
character who owns one good jacket and uses it is more specific than a character
who has just bought one.

## Gear and weapons describe the world, not just the man

The asset column carries the badge in a leather wallet, a belt with a machined
buckle, two pouches, a sidearm and a drop-leg holster rig.

The badge is the most narratively loaded object on the sheet. It carries an
emblem and an identifying number, which means it belongs to a specific
institution with enough bureaucracy to number its officers. That single prop
establishes more about the setting than the character himself does.

The weapon is where the register is calibrated most precisely. It is built on a
recognisable semi-automatic pistol so that it reads instantly as a handgun, then
pushed just far enough with panel lines and an indicator strip to place the
story slightly ahead of the present. Design it from scratch and the audience
spends a beat working out what it is. Leave it entirely stock and the world
loses its future tense. The useful position is the narrow one between those.

The holster rig matters for a reason that has nothing to do with the audience.
Straps, buckles and mounting points are the parts a modeller cannot invent and a
rigger cannot fake, and they are exactly the parts most often left vague. Drawing
them properly here is what makes the sheet usable rather than merely attractive.

## The pair is the point

This sheet and Tagger share a layout, a lighting setup, a background value and
an asset column. That consistency is not a template applied out of habit. When
several characters are designed for one production, the sheets have to be
comparable, because the questions asked of them are comparative: does this
character read against that one, are they the same height, do their palettes
collide, do they look as though they belong in the same world.

A set of design sheets is a body of work rather than a series of pictures, and
it is judged as one.

## What to watch for

- **The shoes.** The clearest example on either sheet of a small decision doing large narrative work.
- **Where the future enters.** Only the weapon and the badge carry it. Everything else is present-day, which is what keeps the world grounded.
- **Wear and use.** Creases, scuffing and the forearm wrap are placed where a body would put them, not scattered for texture.
- **Load paths.** Follow each strap and ask what is holding what up. That is the difference between designed gear and drawn gear.
