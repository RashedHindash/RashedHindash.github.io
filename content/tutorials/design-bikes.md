Most vehicle designs fail long before the rendering stage. They fail because the
wrong shape was committed to on day one and then defended for a week.

## What the video covers

A fast thumbnailing pass for vehicle design: generating forms quickly by working
from photographs of real vehicles and real objects, cutting and reassembling
their parts into new silhouettes, and arriving at a concept worth developing
before any time is spent stylising or detailing it. Recorded start to finish and
sped up, with no commentary.

## Thumbnailing is a search, not a drawing

A thumbnail is not a small drawing. It is one sample taken from the space of
possible designs, and its purpose is to be discarded.

That reframing changes the economics of the work. If a thumbnail is an attempt
at the answer, then producing it slowly and carefully is rational, and throwing
it away is a loss. If a thumbnail is a probe, then the only figure that matters
is how many you can take before you have to decide, and anything that slows a
single one down is costing you coverage of the search space.

The practical consequence is that the first pass should feel almost careless.
Speed is not a compromise made against quality here; it is the mechanism by
which quality is found, because a good design is usually the fifteenth idea
rather than the first, and the only way to reach the fifteenth is to make the
first fourteen cheap.

## Building from photographed parts

The method on display is to work from photographs of vehicles and objects that
already exist, cutting out their components and recombining them.

Take the example in the recording: a photograph of a 1930s sedan supplies the
wheels, the grille, the chrome fenders and the running-board line, and those
parts are then compressed and reassembled into a low speedster form that never
existed. The result is a new vehicle constructed almost entirely from evidence.

The advantage is that photographic parts arrive with things that are slow and
difficult to draw and instantly obvious when wrong: correct perspective, correct
material response, correct manufacturing logic, correct proportional
relationships between a wheel and an arch and a body panel. You inherit all of
it at no cost, which frees your attention for the only question that actually
requires a designer, which is what overall form this vehicle should have.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Real objects carry the marks of having been
manufactured for a purpose: panel gaps, fasteners, the places where two
materials meet. Invented details rarely have that logic, and their absence is
what makes a fully drawn concept feel weightless.

## The order of operations is the lesson

Silhouette first, then proportion, then stylisation, then detail. That sequence
is not a preference. It is an ordering by cost of change.

The silhouette is nearly free to alter at the start and nearly impossible to
alter once rendering has begun. Detail is the reverse: cheap to add at the end
and worthless when added early, because it will be discarded along with the
shape it was sitting on. Detailing an unresolved silhouette is the single most
common way to lose a day of concept work, and it is seductive precisely because
it feels productive while it is happening.

Stylisation belongs late for the same reason. Exaggerating proportions is a
decision that only makes sense once you know which proportions you have.

## What to watch for

- **How quickly a form is abandoned.** The willingness to discard is the skill being demonstrated.
- **Which parts are photographic and which are painted.** The join between them is where the technique either holds or collapses.
- **Perspective consistency.** Parts pulled from different photographs have different vanishing points, and reconciling them is most of the work.
- **When detail arrives.** Track how late it is, and notice that the design was already resolved before it did.
