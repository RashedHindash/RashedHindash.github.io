This is the part of film production where the world is decided, and it happens
before anything is built.

## What the video covers

The refinement of an environment image, taken from real footage and converted
from a morning scene into a night scene. Set design and visualisation of the
foreground, midground and background, the lighting and mood that follow from
that, and the matte painting technique that keeps a heavily altered image
reading as photographic. The setting is a near-future district in the register
of grounded science fiction. Recorded start to finish and sped up, with no
commentary.

## Why this happens before any 3D work

Concept art is often presented as the pretty stage at the front of a pipeline.
It is more accurate to describe it as the stage where mistakes are affordable.

A painting can be relit, restaged, emptied out or rebuilt in an afternoon. The
same changes made after a set has been modelled, textured and lit cost weeks and
involve several departments. So the purpose of the concept image is to spend
every argument about the world while the argument is still cheap: what time of
day this is, where the light comes from, how far away the city sits, what the
audience should look at first, and how the place is supposed to feel.

The image that comes out of this stage is not a picture of the film. It is the
brief for the people who will build it, and it is judged on whether it answers
questions rather than on whether it is beautiful.

## Time of day is a lighting decision, not a colour grade

Turning morning into night is the exercise, and it is instructive because the
naive version fails so clearly. Cooling and darkening the image produces a
daytime photograph in a dim room. It never reads as night, and the reason is
that night is a structural condition rather than a tonal one.

In daylight there is one dominant source, the sky fills the shadows, and almost
every surface in frame is reflecting light. At night the sun is gone and nothing
replaces it, so the scene becomes lit by many small sources that are inside the
frame instead of outside it. Windows, signage, vehicle lamps and instrument
glow stop being details and start being the light. Surfaces that were the
brightest things in the image become the darkest, and objects that were dark
become emissive.

Contrast behaves differently too. Without a sky to fill them, shadows go to
black much faster, which is why a night image can carry an enormous value range
and still read as dark. Atmosphere becomes visible rather than invisible,
because haze is now catching light from sources within the shot instead of being
washed flat by the sun. And colour separates: a warm point source and a cool
ambient no longer average out into daylight, so the palette splits along
temperature lines that simply do not exist at noon.

Rebuilding the lighting from those principles is what makes the conversion
convincing. Filtering the original never can.

## Depth is built in planes

The set is designed as three distinct layers, each with a different job.

The **background** establishes scale and place: a skyline, a containing
structure, the sense that this district continues past the edges of the frame.
It is the least detailed layer and the most atmospheric, because distance is
communicated by loss of contrast rather than by loss of information.

The **midground** carries the story. It is where the barrier and its signage
sit, and it is the layer that tells you the relationship between the figure and
the city: that there is a line here, that the place beyond it is not available.
An environment without a midground statement is scenery. With one, it is a
situation.

The **foreground** exists to frame and to place the camera. The ground plane and
the mass at the edge of frame give the eye something near to measure the
distance against, and they establish that the viewer is standing somewhere
specific rather than floating.

Staging follows from those layers. The figure is placed against the brightest
part of the background so the silhouette reads instantly, set back from the
foreground so the depth is legible, and turned away from the camera so the
audience looks where the character looks. None of that is an accident of
composition; it is set design doing the work that blocking would otherwise have
to do later.

## Matte painting keeps it honest

The technique underneath all of this is matte painting: real photographic
material, cut, extended and painted into, so the result inherits the texture and
imperfection of a recorded image while depicting something that was never
recorded.

The discipline is integration rather than rendering. Painted elements have to
match the source in perspective, in edge quality, in grain and in the way light
falls across them. A cleanly painted addition sitting inside photographic
material is more obvious than a roughly painted one that matches, because the
eye detects the difference in noise and softness long before it evaluates the
draughtsmanship.

That is the underlying claim of the whole exercise. What makes an image feel
real is not detail. It is consistency, and photographic source material is
simply the fastest way to be consistent about a very large number of things at
once.

## What to watch for

- **What becomes the light source.** Track which surfaces stop reflecting and start emitting as the scene turns over.
- **The value range at night.** Notice how wide it stays, and that the darkness comes from where the values sit rather than from the image being dim.
- **Atmospheric perspective.** Follow contrast falling off with distance. It is doing more to build depth than any detail is.
- **Edges and grain.** Look at where painted material meets photographic material, and how much work goes into making that boundary disappear.
- **Foreground silhouettes.** They are almost pure black, and they are the cheapest depth in the frame.
